Hook

Think of a car recall, but for aircraft.

When a safety problem is found, the US aviation regulator, the FAA, orders airlines to inspect or fix it. That order is called an Airworthiness Directive.

Before it becomes final, it is published as a proposal. And proposals change: some are rewritten, some replace older orders, some are cancelled years later.

App open on the Ask screen. Torq is waving on the welcome card.
Point at the header: 431 notices, 1,302 pages.

The problem

An aircraft engineer has to know which of these apply to the aircraft in front of them. Today that means reading government notices one by one. Hundreds of them, thousands of pages.

Three things make it hard. A cancellation is a separate notice from the original. Each notice names exact models: an A350-941 is not "any A350". And some cover only certain serial numbers that are not in the summary.

Stay on the Ask screen.
Optional: open the model dropdown to show the 769 models.

Why not a chatbot?

A normal AI chatbot sounds sure of itself even when it is guessing. It might tell an engineer to inspect a part for a proposal that was cancelled, or say a notice applies because the name looks similar.

In a hangar, a wrong answer means an unnecessary repair, or a missed safety check.

No clicks. Look at the judges.
1:05
Our solution

So we built Torque Stripe. The rule is simple: the AI reads, the rules decide, the engineer signs.

Every safety decision is made by fixed rules in code. The answer quotes the official notice word for word, with the page it came from. And when it cannot be sure, it says so.

A torque stripe is the paint line a mechanic draws across a bolt once it is checked. If the line breaks, you stop and look.

Torque Stripe does that for answers. The AI reads. The rules decide. The engineer signs. Thank you.

If judges ask

Is this using AI?

Yes, but only to reword answers. Which notice, which version, whether your model is covered, and when to refuse are all fixed rules. Every reworded sentence is checked against the source, and if it fails, the original text is shown.

What about serial numbers?

The FAA summaries in this dataset do not list serial numbers, so for "certain units" it says it cannot confirm and links the official page. Our sample engine shows serial ranges working as rules on labelled sample documents. In production we would read the full notices.

How fast is it?

About a tenth of a millisecond per question on average across 1,302 pages, and under 10 milliseconds at worst. The brief asked for under 3 seconds.

Is this an Airbus product?

No. It was built for Airbus Challenge #4 at Autonomous SG 2026. It is not an official Airbus or FAA product.

What would production need?

Company sign-in with roles, a daily sync of new FAA and EASA notices, the rules engine as a hosted service, signed reports stored in a tamper-proof audit log, and the 42-question test as a release gate.

Before you start

  • Open torque-stripe.vercel.app in a full-screen browser window (it runs in the browser, no server needed).
  • Close the guided tour, or keep it for the first 10 seconds if time allows.
  • Model field set to AS350B3.
  • Zoom the browser so the demo chips are readable from the back of the room.
